# Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171>

Wang, Wei, Schuurmans, Le, Chi, Narang, Chowdhery, Zhou. Google Research,
Brain Team. ICLR 2023.

## Core ideas

Self-consistency is a **decoding strategy**, not a prompting technique.
The prompt is exactly the chain-of-thought prompt from 2201.11903 and
does not change. What changes is what you do with the decoder: instead
of greedily decoding one path, sample a diverse set of paths and then
keep the answer that most of them agree on.

The paper calls this **sample-and-marginalize**. The reasoning path is
treated as a latent variable you do not actually want: you sample pairs
of path and answer, then marginalize the path out by taking a plain
majority vote over the answers. The path was only ever a device for
reaching the answer, so once the answers are in hand the paths are
discarded.

The hypothesis underneath is the one sentence worth remembering:
**correct reasoning processes, even when they differ, tend to agree on
the final answer more than incorrect ones do.** There are many ways to
be right about a hard problem and they converge; there are more ways to
be wrong and they scatter. Agreement is therefore evidence, and it costs
nothing beyond the samples already paid for.

Several findings sharpen this.

- **Plain majority vote is enough.** Weighting each path by its model
  probability performs about the same as an unweighted vote, provided
  the probability is length-normalized. Unnormalized weighting and
  weighted averaging are both worse.
- **The reason plain voting suffices is a calibration failure.** The
  model assigns near-identical normalized probabilities to good and bad
  paths alike, so it cannot separate its own correct solutions from its
  incorrect ones. The paper says this outright, and notes it is why
  earlier work had to train a separate verifier or re-ranker.
- **Diversity is the mechanism.** Self-consistency beats sample-and-rank,
  which samples the same number of paths and keeps the highest
  log-probability one. It beats beam search, and self-consistency run on
  top of beam search is worse than self-consistency run on top of
  sampling, because beam search produces paths that resemble each other.
  If the paths are not diverse, there is nothing for agreement to mean.
- **It beats ensembling the prompt.** Permuting the exemplar order, or
  writing several prompt sets and voting across them, both help far less
  than sampling multiple paths from one prompt. This is a self-ensemble
  over a single model, not a model ensemble and not a prompt ensemble.
- **It is unsupervised and needs nothing extra.** No training, no
  finetuning, no verifier, no re-ranker, no additional human annotation.
  It works off the shelf on top of whatever model is already in place.

Three robustness results matter more than the headline. It is insensitive
to the sampling parameters, so temperature and top-k do not need tuning.
It **recovers from a deliberately corrupted prompt**: the authors
scrambled the numbers inside the exemplar chains, which lowered greedy
accuracy, and voting not only closed that gap but passed the clean greedy
baseline. And it helps on tasks where adding a chain of thought had
actually *hurt* relative to standard prompting, turning the regression
into a gain.

It also works with zero-shot chain of thought, and with equation-shaped
intermediate steps rather than natural-language ones, though the gain is
smaller for equations. The stated reason is worth carrying: equations are
short, and short outputs leave less room for the diversity the method
runs on.

Finally, and separately from accuracy: **the level of agreement
correlates with accuracy.** Low agreement marks the questions the model
is likely getting wrong. The paper puts it as self-consistency conferring
some ability to know when it does not know.

## What problem it solves

Chain of thought commits to a single path. This paper's own framing of
why that is costly, in its introduction, is that greedy decoding is
plagued by repetitiveness and local-optimality while any single sampled
generation is stochastic. Separately, the limitation 2201.11903 stated in
its own discussion, that there is no guarantee of a correct reasoning
path, has no remedy available inside a single generation.

The remedies that did exist cost supervision. One line of work trained a
verifier to score candidate solutions; another collected human
annotations to train a re-ranker. Both work, and both require the
expensive thing. Self-consistency reaches the same effect using only the
decoder that ships with the model, which is why it landed as the default
upgrade to chain of thought rather than as a result you would have to
fund.

## Limitations

- **Cost.** It multiplies computation by the number of paths. The paper
  offers its own mitigation: performance saturates quickly, so a small
  number of paths captures most of the gain, and it explicitly suggests
  starting there rather than at the count used in the experiments.
- **It requires a fixed answer set.** Marginalization is a vote, and a
  vote must decide when two answers are the same. The paper is direct
  that the method applies only where the final answer comes from a fixed
  set, and that extending it to open-ended generation would require a
  good consistency metric between generations, which it does not supply.
  This is the boundary that decides whether you can use it at all.
- **Voting selects answers, not reasoning.** The paper's own example has
  the model reach the right answer through a path containing invented
  figures. Agreement on the conclusion says nothing about whether the
  steps were sound, and the ethics statement restricts the rationales to
  inspection rather than to grounds for a decision.
- **Small models gain least**, because the underlying ability has to be
  present before diverse attempts can agree on it.
- **Diversity depends on having room to vary.** Short intermediate
  outputs give the sampler less to work with, which is why the equation
  variant gains less than the natural-language one.

## What this means in practice

1. **What the paper calls a decoding strategy is, for you, an
   orchestration pattern.** No production API exposes sample many paths
   and marginalize. You issue N independent requests, parse an answer out
   of each, and aggregate in your own code. The shape of the cost follows
   from that: N times the tokens and N times the rate limit pressure, but
   roughly one call of latency if you fan out concurrently instead of
   looping. The work is network-bound, so a thread pool is the whole
   implementation.

2. **The equality check is the design, and it decides feasibility.**
   Before reaching for voting, answer one question: what does it mean for
   two of these outputs to be the same answer. A number, a label, an
   enum, a boolean, a chosen file path all have an obvious answer. Prose,
   a plan, a diff, or generated code do not, and the paper is explicit
   that the method does not cover them. If you cannot write the
   comparison, you do not need a vote, you need a judge.

3. **Ship the agreement level, not just the winner.** Agreement
   correlates with accuracy, which makes it a confidence signal you get
   for free from samples already paid for. Returning the pair of answer
   and agreement fraction turns a voting step into a routing decision:
   high agreement proceeds automatically, low agreement escalates to a
   stronger model or to a person. In production this is usually worth
   more than the accuracy gain, because it tells you *which* answers to
   distrust rather than making all of them slightly better.

4. **Do not rank by the model's own confidence.** Sample-and-rank by log
   probability underperformed voting, and the aggregation study says why:
   the model scores good and bad paths about the same. The modern form of
   this mistake is asking the model to pick its own best answer out of
   several, or to report a confidence score, and then trusting the reply.
   Agreement between independent samples is a measurement; self-reported
   confidence is another generation.

5. **If you vote, you must sample.** Diversity is the mechanism, not a
   side effect. Running N calls at temperature zero returns N copies of
   one answer and an agreement fraction that means nothing, at N times
   the price. The beam search comparison is the paper's version of this
   warning.

6. **Start with a handful of paths and measure saturation.** Most of the
   gain arrives early. Treat the count as a cost knob with diminishing
   returns and find where your own task flattens, rather than adopting a
   number from a paper whose task set is not yours. Prompt caching makes
   the shared prefix cheap across the fan-out, so the marginal cost of
   one more vote is mostly its own output tokens, reasoning included.

7. **Majority is the default, not the rule.** Plain majority matched
   probability weighting here, so start there. But the rule encodes what
   it costs to be wrong: require unanimity when a false positive is
   expensive, accept any single yes when a miss is expensive. That choice
   belongs to the application, not to the paper.

8. **This composes with reasoning models, at a real multiple.** A model
   that already spends a long internal path per call is not doing this;
   self-consistency is external and orthogonal, and it still applies. But
   the per-call cost it multiplies is now much larger, so the arithmetic
   that made voting cheap on short outputs no longer holds automatically.
   Price it before assuming it.

## What it leads to next

- **Tree of Thoughts (2305.10601)** is the next move once one path is
  accepted as not enough. Self-consistency samples paths that never see
  each other and reconciles them only at the end. Tree of Thoughts lets
  the search branch, evaluates partial paths as they develop, and
  backtracks. The difference is whether the paths communicate.

Read against **2201.11903**, which this directly extends. Chain of
thought made the reasoning visible, and self-consistency is the first
answer to the limitation that visibility alone does not make it correct.
The other answer, checking the work with a separate pass rather than with
a vote, is the whole of `03-self-correction`.

## Connection to the lab

`papers.yaml` maps this paper to `labs/track-1-patterns/04-parallelization`,
and here the mapping is close to literal. The voting half of that lab is
this paper's mechanism: `vote` runs the same question n times
concurrently and collects the answers, and `majority` returns the modal
answer together with the fraction of votes that agreed.

**That return type is the paper's second contribution.** `majority`
returning a pair rather than a string is not a convenience, it is the
uncertainty estimate: agreement correlates with accuracy, so the fraction
is a confidence signal that costs nothing extra. A version returning only
the winner would throw away half of what the sampling bought.

**Lab 04's framing of aggregation is this paper's aggregation study
turned into an engineering rule.** The lab says the aggregation rule is a
design decision rather than an afterthought, and lists majority,
unanimity, and any-yes as encoding different costs of being wrong. The
paper supplies the empirical half: unweighted majority is a strong
default because the model's own probabilities do not separate good paths
from bad ones.

**The lab's cost warning is this paper's only real limitation.** N votes
means N times the tokens and a fan-out wide enough to trip a rate limit
that a serial version never would. The paper's answer, that performance
saturates quickly so a small count captures most of the gain, is the
thing to bring to the lab's own exercise of raising the vote count and
asking whether agreement rose enough to justify the bill.

Elsewhere in the sibling repository:

- **Lab 02, prompt chaining.** The other response to the same
  chain-of-thought limitation, and worth holding side by side. A gate is
  a deterministic check on one output, cheap and specific. A vote is a
  statistical check across many outputs, expensive and general. A gate
  catches a malformed or empty intermediate result; a vote catches a
  well-formed and wrong one. They fail on opposite things.
- **Lab 06, evaluator-optimizer.** Where you go when the equality check
  does not exist. Voting needs answers that can be compared; prose and
  code need something that reads and judges them instead.
- **Lab 03, routing, and lab 14, human in the loop.** The two natural
  consumers of the agreement fraction. Low agreement is the cleanest
  escalation trigger this course offers, because it is measured rather
  than self-reported.
- **Lab 13, subagents.** Related shape, different intent, and easy to
  confuse. Voting fans out over the *same* work to compare answers;
  subagents fan out over *different* work to cover more of it. Only the
  first produces an agreement number that means anything.
- **Labs 15 and 16, evaluation and observability.** Agreement per run is
  worth logging as a first-class field. It is a leading indicator: a
  drift downward says the inputs are getting harder or the prompt has
  decayed, and it shows up before the accuracy metric does.
  `common/cost.py` is where the multiplier becomes visible.
- **Lab 17, guardrails.** A voted answer is a safer answer, and the
  reasoning behind it is no safer than it was. The paper's example of a
  correct answer reached through invented figures is the case to hold in
  mind before displaying a winning path as justification.
