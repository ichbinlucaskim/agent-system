# ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629>

Yao, Zhao, Yu, Du, Shafran, Narasimhan, Cao. Princeton University and
Google Research, Brain Team. ICLR 2023.

## Core ideas

The formal move is one line and it is the part worth keeping. Augment the
agent's action space to include language: the available actions become
the environment's actions plus the space of language. An action taken in
the language half is a **thought**. It does not affect the environment
and produces no observation. All it does is compose information from the
current context and append itself to that context, so that later
reasoning and later actions have it available.

**A thought is an action with no side effects.** Everything else follows
from that. Because thoughts are free in the world and expensive only in
tokens, they can be inserted anywhere in a trajectory without changing
what the agent does to anything outside itself.

Interleaving the two halves buys a benefit in each direction. *Reason to
act*: the model creates, maintains, and adjusts a plan, decomposes a
goal, injects commonsense the environment does not supply, extracts what
matters from a noisy observation, tracks which subgoal is finished, and
handles exceptions. *Act to reason*: the model fetches a fact instead of
recalling one, which is the direct fix for the diagnosis the paper opens
with, that chain-of-thought reasoning is a **static black box**, running
on internal representations with no grounding, and therefore prone to
hallucinated facts and to propagating an early error forward.

The density of thoughts is a per-task decision rather than a fixed
format. For knowledge-heavy reasoning the trajectory alternates
thought, action, and observation at every step. For decision-making
tasks with long action horizons, thoughts appear **sparsely** at the
positions that matter, and the model is left to decide for itself when to
think.

The failure analysis is more useful than the scores.

- **Chain of thought's dominant failure is hallucination**, and it also
  shows a higher rate of right answers reached through fabricated
  reasoning. Interacting with an external source largely removes that
  failure mode.
- **ReAct's dominant failure is a reasoning error**, and it makes more of
  them than chain of thought does. The paper is explicit about the cause:
  the structural constraint of alternating with actions and observations
  reduces the model's flexibility in formulating reasoning steps. This is
  a trade, not a free upgrade.
- **One named error pattern is the repetitive loop**, where the model
  regenerates its previous thought and action, fails to reason about what
  should come next, and cannot get out.
- **A non-informative search derails the run.** A meaningful share of
  failures come from a search returning nothing useful, after which the
  model has a hard time recovering and reformulating.

Two results beyond the main comparison. Combining with self-consistency
in **both directions** beat either alone: fall back to sampled internal
reasoning when acting fails to produce an answer within its step budget,
and fall back to acting when the majority answer among sampled reasoning
paths is weak, meaning internal knowledge does not confidently support
the task. And **finetuning reverses the ranking**. Prompted, ReAct is
hardest for small models because they must learn to reason and to act
from context at once; finetuned on a modest number of trajectories it
becomes the best of the four methods. The paper's explanation is worth
carrying: finetuning on plain answers or on chains of thought teaches the
model to memorize facts, while finetuning on acting teaches it how to go
get them, which generalizes better.

The paper also claims interpretability as a deliverable, not a side
effect: a human can tell which claims came from the model and which came
from the environment, and can steer a run by editing a thought.

## What problem it solves

The two halves existed separately and each was broken in its own way.
Reasoning without grounding produces confident text with no way to check
a fact or update on the world. Acting without reasoning predicts the next
action from context but keeps no working memory, does not decompose a
goal, and does not reason abstractly about what it is doing, so it loses
track of state and repeats hallucinated actions.

ReAct is the claim that these are not two research areas but two halves
of one loop, and that the join is nearly free: it needs a prompt format
and a few demonstrations, not a new architecture and not training.

## Limitations

- **Groundedness is bought with flexibility.** More reasoning errors than
  an unconstrained chain, because the reasoning has to fit between
  actions.
- **The repetitive loop is a real failure mode**, and it is the kind that
  looks healthy from the outside: the agent keeps producing well-formed
  steps and never converges.
- **The agent is only as good as its observations.** A weak retrieval
  surface is not a small tax, it is a derailment the model rarely
  recovers from.
- **Demonstrations do not scale to large action spaces.** The paper's own
  conclusion states it: complex tasks with many actions need more
  demonstrations to learn from, and those demonstrations run past the
  input length limit of in-context learning.
- **Attaching an action space to a model is genuinely dangerous**, and
  the ethics statement is unusually concrete about it. The experiments
  were kept safe by construction, restricted to sources without private
  information and to action spaces containing nothing harmful, with no
  ability to actually buy anything or edit anything.

## What this means in practice

1. **This is the harness answer to tool use, and it is why nearly every
   agent framework has this shape.** Nothing is trained. The tool set
   lives in the prompt and in the loop, so tools can be added, removed,
   or changed between one call and the next, and a new tool costs a
   schema and a description rather than a training run. Hold this against
   2302.04761, which is the learned answer: **when tools change often or
   their number is fluid, the harness wins; the learned route is only
   worth considering when the tool set is fixed and the hard part is
   judging when to call.** In practice it is almost always the former.

2. **Take the thought-is-an-action-without-side-effects distinction
   literally when you design an action space.** Split your actions by
   whether they change anything outside the process. That line is where
   approval gates go, where retries are safe, and where a step can be
   abandoned for free. It is also the line that 2305.10601 assumes
   without saying so, which is why search over thoughts is cheap and
   search over actions is not.

3. **Detect the repetitive loop explicitly. A step budget will not do
   it.** The paper named this failure, and the shape of it is that the
   agent burns its entire allowance producing the same call. Hash recent
   action and observation pairs and stop when the window stops changing.
   That is a separate stop condition from the budget, and the run should
   report which one fired.

4. **Observation quality is the ceiling on the whole loop.** A tool
   result is the input to the next reasoning step, so a vague result
   poisons everything after it. This is the practical reason tool design
   deserves as much effort as prompt design, and why an error message is
   a prompt: what you return after a failure is the next thing the model
   reads.

5. **Route between internal and external knowledge rather than choosing
   one.** The best configurations were the two backoffs, and the trigger
   for one of them is exactly the agreement fraction from 2203.11171: low
   agreement among sampled answers means the model does not know, and
   that is when it should go look. High agreement means fetching is spend
   for nothing. This is the most directly implementable idea in the
   paper.

6. **Sparse thinking is a knob, not a compromise.** Thinking at every
   step of a long action sequence is waste. Deciding where thought is
   worth paying for is the modern equivalent of choosing an effort level
   and choosing where to interleave thinking with tool calls.

7. **Few-shot trajectories hit the context wall, and that pressure is
   what makes tool descriptions matter.** If you cannot afford to
   demonstrate every action, each tool has to carry its own instructions
   for when it applies. The demonstration budget is why trigger
   conditions in a description outperform examples in a prompt.

8. **Keep the trace, because it is the product as much as the answer
   is.** Being able to separate what the model believed from what the
   environment returned is what makes a failed run diagnosable, and
   editing a thought mid-run is the cheapest steering mechanism available.

## What it leads to next

- **Toolformer (2302.04761)** takes the other route: instead of putting
  tool use in the prompt, learn it into the weights. Read the two
  together, because the choice between them is the real content.
- **Reflexion (2303.11366)** keeps the loop and adds memory across
  attempts, so a failed trajectory becomes an input to the next one. It
  is the direct answer to the repetitive-loop failure named here.
- **SWE-agent (2405.15793)** takes the observation-quality finding
  seriously and designs the interface itself as the variable.
- **Voyager (2305.16291)** carries the loop into an open-ended
  environment where the actions accumulate into a skill library.

Backwards, this extends **2201.11903**: chain of thought produced the
thought, and this paper gives the thought somewhere to act.

## Connection to the lab

`papers.yaml` maps this paper to two labs, and the pair is the paper
split in half.

**`labs/track-1-patterns/01-augmented-llm` is one turn of it.** That lab
builds the single augmented call: retrieval into the prompt, a tool
described by a schema, memory as the history you choose to resend, and a
loop driven by `stop_reason`. Its central point is the protocol split,
that the model never executes anything but emits a `tool_use` block, your
code runs the action, and a `tool_result` goes back. That split is the
engineering form of this paper's action space: the model proposes, the
harness disposes, and the boundary between them is where safety lives.

The lab's distinction between retrieval and tools is this paper's *act to
reason* stated operationally. A document holds what was true when it was
written; a tool reads what is true now. The paper's grounding argument is
about exactly that gap, and its failure analysis shows what happens when
the model answers from stale internal knowledge instead of going to look.

**`labs/track-3-autonomy/12-agent-loop` is the rest of it.** That lab is
where the model, not your code, decides what happens next, and almost all
of its content is limits: independent step, cost, and wall-clock budgets
that fail differently, whichever binds first stopping the run, and the
run reporting which one it was.

Its `detect_no_progress`, which hashes recent action and observation
pairs and stops when the recent window is all repeats, is a direct
implementation of the repetitive loop this paper named. The lab's
observation that a stuck agent does not crash but stays confident is the
same finding stated from the operator's side.

`run_tool_with_retry` splits the two responses to a tool failure that
this paper leaves implicit: retrying is your code deciding to try again,
and returning the error as a tool result is handing the decision to the
model. The paper's non-informative-search failure is the case where the
second one matters and the error text is what determines whether the run
recovers.

Elsewhere in the sibling repository:

- **Lab 07, tool design and the agent-computer interface.** The response
  to this paper's observation-quality finding. If a non-informative
  result derails a run, the wording of the tool that produced it is not a
  detail.
- **Lab 14, human in the loop.** Where the side-effect line from point
  two becomes a policy: which actions run automatically, which need

- **Lab 04, parallelization.** Supplies the agreement fraction that the

- **Lab 16, observability.** The trace is the deliverable here, and what
  makes it useful is that thoughts and observations are distinguishable
  in it.
