# Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903>

Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, Zhou. Google Research,
Brain Team. NeurIPS 2022.

## Core ideas

The method is one change: each few-shot exemplar becomes a triple of
input, chain of thought, and output, instead of a pair of input and
output. A chain of thought is a series of intermediate natural language
steps that lead to the final answer. Nothing is finetuned, no training
data is collected, and one checkpoint keeps serving every task.

The change is to the shape of the *output*, not the input. Instructions,
task descriptions, and learned prompts all improve what goes into the
model. This work is orthogonal to all of them: it changes what the model
is shown to emit before committing to an answer.

Because the steps are emitted as tokens, the model reads its own
intermediate work while producing the final answer, and a problem that
needs more steps naturally consumes more computation than one that needs
fewer. The paper lists this as the first attractive property, alongside
an interpretable window into how an answer arose, applicability to
anything expressible in language, and the fact that it needs nothing but
prompting.

The ablations carry more weight than the headline result, because they
are what rules out the cheaper explanations. Three variants were tried
against plain chain of thought:

- **Equation only.** Emit the equation, then the answer. Does not recover
  the gain on multi-step word problems, though it does help when the
  problem is one or two steps and the equation reads straight off the
  question.
- **Variable compute only.** Emit a sequence of dots matched in length to
  the equation that would be needed, then the answer. Performs about like
  the baseline. Extra token budget by itself is not the mechanism.
- **Chain of thought after the answer.** Same tokens, reversed order.
  Performs about like the baseline. The model gains nothing from a chain
  it has already answered past.

Taken together: it is the sequential natural-language steps, positioned
*before* the answer, that do the work. Not compute, not knowledge
activation, not translation into a formal notation.

Two further properties matter. The gain is an emergent ability of model
scale, absent in small models and appearing only in large ones; below
that scale the models produced fluent but illogical chains and did worse
than standard prompting. And the gain is robust to who wrote the
exemplars, to a deliberately terser writing style, to exemplars sampled
from a training set instead of hand-written, and to exemplar order and
count. There is variance across prompts, as expected of a prompting
method, but every variant beat the baseline.

## What problem it solves

Two prior lines of work each carried a blocking cost. Training or
finetuning on natural language rationales works, but producing a large
set of high quality rationales is far more expensive than collecting
input-output pairs. Standard few-shot prompting is nearly free, but it
does poorly on reasoning tasks and, worse, its curve stays flat as models
grow: scaling alone did not fix it.

Chain-of-thought prompting takes the rationale idea and pays for it with
a handful of hand-written exemplars instead of a dataset. The consequence
worth carrying is the one the paper states plainly: standard prompting
gives only a *lower bound* on what a model can do. A task that looks
unsolvable may be a task whose output format was wrong.

## Limitations

The paper's own list, which is unusually candid and worth keeping:

- Chain of thought imitates the shape of human reasoning. Whether the
  network is reasoning is left open, explicitly.
- **There is no guarantee of a correct reasoning path.** Wrong chains can
  produce right answers and right-looking chains can produce wrong ones.
  In the manual error analysis, some correct answers came from chains
  that reached them coincidentally, and among wrong answers a large share
  were nearly correct while the rest broke down in semantic understanding
  or coherence.
- Annotation cost is negligible for a few exemplars but would be
  prohibitive at finetuning scale.
- Emergence at scale means the method costs real money to serve, and
  offers nothing to small models.

Two more read out of the results rather than the discussion. Gains are
largest where the baseline is worst and shrink to nothing, or turn
slightly negative, on single-step problems. And on one of the
commonsense benchmarks the gain was minimal, so "this is a reasoning
task" is not a reliable predictor that the method will pay.

## What this means in practice

The reason to read this paper now is not to learn the technique. The
technique has been absorbed into the models: reasoning models emit
intermediate steps natively, and the labs in the sibling repository
control that with an effort setting rather than with hand-written chains.
What survives is the finding, and the finding constrains how you build.

1. **Do not hand-roll a chain into a prompt for a model that already
   thinks.** Pasting step-by-step scaffolding or exemplar chains in front
   of a reasoning model duplicates machinery that is already there, and
   you pay for the duplicate in tokens and latency. Reach for explicit
   exemplars when you need a *specific* decomposition the model would not
   choose on its own, not to obtain step-by-step behaviour in general.

2. **Intermediate tokens are real tokens, and they are where the budget
   goes.** They are billed as output, they add latency in proportion to
   their length, and they count against the response ceiling. In this
   repository `common/client.py` sets a conservative `DEFAULT_MAX_TOKENS`
   precisely because that ceiling bounds thinking plus visible text, and
   `labs/00-setup/README.md` names the symptom: a `stop_reason` of
   `max_tokens` on a request whose ceiling was sized for the answer
   alone. That is the most common way this paper's finding shows up as a
   production bug.

3. **Order is load-bearing, and structured output is where you break
   it.** The chain-of-thought-after-answer ablation is the single most
   useful result here for an engineer. If you define a response schema
   whose answer field is emitted before its reasoning field, or ask for a
   verdict and then a justification, you have reproduced the ablation
   that does not work: the answer was already fixed when the reasoning
   was written. Put the reasoning field first, or let thinking finish
   before the structured block starts.

4. **The chain is not an explanation, and must not be used as one.** The
   paper says there is no guarantee the path is correct, and the error
   analysis shows both failure directions. So: do not show it to an end
   user as a justification, do not keep it as the audit record of why a
   decision was made, and never accept an answer because its reasoning
   read well. Verify the answer against something outside the model.

5. **Turn it on by difficulty, not globally.** The gain concentrates on
   hard multi-step inputs and rounds to zero on one-step ones. Routing
   cheap inputs to a shallow call and expensive ones to a deep call is
   the same trade the paper measured, and it is lab 03 in the sibling
   repository.

6. **Parse the answer deterministically, never the chain.** The chain
   varies in length and style by design, and the robustness experiments
   confirm that its style shifts with the prompt while answer quality
   holds. Depend on a terminal marker or a structured field. A regular
   expression over the reasoning is a parser built on the least stable
   part of the output.

7. **Voting is the cheap upgrade, at a linear price.** Sampling several
   chains and taking the majority answer improves on greedy decoding, and
   the paper itself points forward to it. It multiplies cost by the
   number of samples, which is a decision to make deliberately rather
   than a default.

## What it leads to next

- **Self-consistency (2203.11171)** replaces greedy decoding with
  sampling many chains and taking the majority final answer. It is the
  direct response to the "no guarantee of a correct path" limitation, on
  the bet that wrong paths disagree with each other while right ones
  converge.
- **Tree of Thoughts (2305.10601)** stops treating the chain as a single
  linear draft and searches over branching steps with explicit evaluation
  and backtracking.
- **ReAct (2210.03629)** interleaves the steps with actions against an
  environment, so a step can fetch a fact instead of recalling one. That
  is the bridge from this topic to the agent loop, and it is why
  `01-reasoning` comes before `02-acting-and-tools` in the default
  reading path.

## Connection to the lab

`papers.yaml` maps this paper to `labs/track-1-patterns/02-prompt-chaining`,
and the mapping is best read as a contrast rather than as an
implementation.

**Where the decomposition lives.** Chain of thought keeps the
decomposition inside one call: the model chooses the steps, in tokens,
and you see them only after the fact. Prompt chaining moves the
decomposition into your code: you choose the steps, each is its own call
with its own instruction set and output format, and the sequence is fixed
before the model runs. Lab 02's background makes the same point from the
other side, that the sequence lives in your code and not in the model's
judgment, which is what makes it predictable, debuggable, and cheap to
test.

**The gate exists because of this paper's limitation.** "No guarantee of
correct reasoning paths" is exactly the property that makes an internal
chain unauditable. Lab 02's `gate_requirements` is a deterministic Python
check between two calls, returning `(ok, reason)`, and it is plain Python
on purpose: it is the checkpoint a chain of thought structurally cannot
have, because there is nowhere inside a single generation to stand and
inspect. When you can afford to buy that checkpoint with latency and
tokens, buy it.

**Error compounding runs both ways.** Lab 02 argues that steps which are
each usually right multiply into a chain that is often wrong, and that
gates are how you stop the multiplication. A chain of thought has the
same compounding structure with no gates anywhere in it. That is the
whole design axis between the two: the same decomposition, a different
place to stand.

  the part most easily forgotten.
