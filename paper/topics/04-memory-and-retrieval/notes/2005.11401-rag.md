# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401>

Lewis, Perez, Piktus, Petroni, Karpukhin, Goyal, Küttler, Lewis, Yih,
Rocktäschel, Riedel, Kiela. Facebook AI Research, University College
London, New York University. NeurIPS 2020.

## Core ideas

The framing is the durable part, and it predates every product built on
this idea. A model has **parametric memory**: what its weights learned,
implicit and unindexed. RAG adds **non-parametric memory**: an explicit
document index that can be read, inspected, and replaced. The
contribution is a general recipe for combining the two in a *generative*
model, at a time when retrieval had only been attached to extractive
question answering.

The pieces: a retriever produces a distribution over passages given the
query, a generator produces the output conditioned on the query and a
retrieved passage, and the retrieved document is treated as a **latent
variable** that gets marginalized over the top few candidates. The
retriever is a bi-encoder, so a query and a passage are embedded
separately and matched by inner product, which makes retrieval a
maximum inner product search rather than a scan.

Two formulations differ in where the marginalization happens.
**RAG-Sequence** commits to one document for the whole output.
**RAG-Token** can draw a different document per token, which lets one
answer combine content from several passages. For a one-token output,
such as a classification label, the two are the same thing.

**Training is end-to-end and unsupervised in the retrieval.** No labels
say which document should have been retrieved; the retriever learns from
the generator's loss on the final answer. One practical decision is worth
remembering: the document encoder and the index are held **fixed**, and
only the query encoder and the generator are updated. Re-encoding the
corpus during training would mean rebuilding the index repeatedly, and
the paper reports not needing it.

Three results carry beyond the architecture.

- **Index hot-swapping.** Build the index from an older snapshot of the
  corpus and the model answers as of that date; swap to a newer snapshot
  and it answers as of the new one. Knowledge is updated by replacing
  the index, with **no retraining**. Parametric-only models need further
  training to move.
- **Generation quality, not just retrieval accuracy.** Human raters found
  the retrieval-augmented output more factual and more specific than a
  parametric-only baseline of the same generator, and it produced less
  repetitive language.
- **Retrieving more passages is not uniformly better.** For one
  formulation accuracy rose monotonically with the number of retrieved
  documents; for the other it peaked and then declined. The count is a
  tunable, and the paper varies it at test time without retraining.

## What problem it solves

A model that stores everything in its weights has three problems the
paper names in its first paragraph: it cannot easily expand or revise
what it knows, it cannot show why it answered as it did, and it
hallucinates. These are not accuracy problems, they are *operational*
problems: updating, auditing, and trusting.

Putting the knowledge in an index addresses all three at once. Revision
is replacing a document. Provenance is the passage you retrieved.
Hallucination drops because the answer is conditioned on text that
actually exists.

## Limitations

- **The index is fixed after training.** The document encoder is frozen,
  so improving embeddings later means re-encoding everything, and the
  retriever cannot adapt its passage representations to the task.
- **Retrieval is unsupervised, so nothing guarantees it is right.** The
  retriever is trained by whether the generator produced a good answer,
  which is a weak and indirect signal about whether the right passage was
  found.
- **The knowledge source is not neutral.** The broader-impact section is
  explicit that no external corpus will be entirely factual or free of
  bias, and that grounding moves the trust question rather than
  eliminating it.
- **Retrieval quality bounds everything downstream**, and the paper's own
  ablations show a weaker retriever dragging the whole system down.
- More retrieved passages is not a free improvement, per the result
  above.

## What this means in practice

1. **The parametric and non-parametric split is the idea to keep, and it
   is a design question you answer per fact.** Anything that changes,
   anything you must cite, and anything you might need to delete belongs
   in the index. Anything stable and general can stay in the weights.
   Finetuning to teach a model facts is usually the wrong instrument for
   the same reason the paper gives: you cannot revise or inspect what you
   have baked in.

2. **Hot-swapping is the operational argument, and it is stronger than
   the accuracy argument.** The reason retrieval won as an architecture
   is not that it scored better, it is that a correction is a document
   edit that takes effect on the next request. Any design that requires
   retraining to fix a wrong fact has chosen the wrong tier.

3. **Provenance only counts if it is checkable.** The paper offers the
   retrieved passage as the explanation. That is worth something only if
   the answer is tied to specific passages and a reader can open them.
   Return the identifiers alongside the answer, and treat an answer whose
   citation does not support it as a failure of the same severity as a
   wrong answer.

4. **Retrieval accuracy is a separate metric from answer accuracy, and
   you need both.** The system can retrieve the right passage and answer
   badly, or answer correctly from parametric memory while retrieving
   nothing useful. If you only measure the end-to-end answer you cannot
   tell those apart, and you will tune the wrong half.

5. **The number of retrieved passages is a tuned parameter, not a
   constant.** It was tuned at test time here without retraining, and the
   two formulations behaved differently. More context is not monotonically
   better, which is a finding that has aged extremely well.

6. **Grounding has to be enforced, not assumed.** Nothing in a retrieval
   pipeline stops a model from reading the passages and then answering
   from memory anyway. The instruction to answer only from the provided
   passages, and to decline when they do not cover the question, is load
   bearing.

## What it leads to next

- **MemGPT (2310.08560)** takes the same non-parametric-memory idea and
  changes who is in charge. Here the system retrieves once, before
  generating, on the system's schedule. There the model decides when to
  read and write, and the memory holds the run's own history rather than
  a static corpus.

Sideways, this is the specific tool that 2302.04761 treated as one of
five and that turned out to deserve its own topic. And it is the same
grounding argument 2210.03629 makes from the acting side: fetch the fact
rather than recall it.

## Connection to the lab

`papers.yaml` maps this to two labs, which split cleanly into building
the pipeline and finding out whether it works.

**`labs/track-2-tools-context/08-rag-basics` is this paper's operational
half.** The lab's opening states the case in exactly this paper's terms:
retrieval is the cheapest way to give a model knowledge it lacks, it
updates the moment the source updates, and unlike finetuning it leaves an
audit trail you can point at. That is hot-swapping and provenance,
restated as reasons to build.

The lab adds the thing this paper takes for granted because it trained
its own retriever: **chunking**. A corpus is not a list of passages until
you decide where passages end, and the lab is right that this is the
first real design decision, with a size that is too small losing context
and too large diluting signal.

The lab's insistence on **grounding enforced in the prompt** is point six
above, and it names the failure precisely: retrieval that is not enforced
gives you a model that reads the passages and answers from memory anyway.
Its rule that a citation is only worth something if it is checkable, so
retrieved chunk ids come back with the answer, is point three.

**`labs/track-2-tools-context/09-retrieval-quality` is where the paper's
own weak point gets taken seriously.** This paper's retriever is trained
indirectly and its ablations show retrieval quality bounding the system.
That lab turns that into measurement: recall at k against a labelled set,
which converts every later change from an opinion into a number.

Several of that lab's warnings have no counterpart in the paper and are
the more valuable for it. Resolution: with a small labelled set, one
query is worth a fixed fraction and a gap smaller than that is nothing.
Ties: a ranking that falls back to declaration order makes a lucky
corpus ordering indistinguishable from a real improvement. Tuning: once
you choose a parameter on a set, that set can no longer tell you how good
the result is, so choose on one half and quote the other. Those are the
rules that decide whether a reported retrieval improvement is real.

The lab's point that keyword and vector search **fail in different
directions** — embeddings smear exact identifiers and error codes that
keyword search finds precisely — is the practical correction to reading
this paper as an argument for dense retrieval alone. The paper compares
against a sparse baseline and moves on; the lab makes the combination a
tuned knob with an honest test for whether it is buying anything.
