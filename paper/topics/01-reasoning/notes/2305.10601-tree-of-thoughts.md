# Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601>

Yao, Yu, Zhao, Shafran, Griffiths, Cao, Narasimhan. Princeton University and
Google DeepMind. NeurIPS 2023.

## Core ideas

Tree of Thoughts frames problem solving as **search over a tree of partial
solutions**. A node is a state, meaning the input plus the sequence of
thoughts so far. A **thought** is a coherent language sequence that
serves as one intermediate step. The lineage is explicit and old: Newell
and Simon's characterization of problem solving as search through a
combinatorial problem space, brought forward and given a language model
as its heuristic.

The diagnosis is that a language model decodes left to right, token by
token, and therefore cannot do two things. **Locally** it does not
explore alternative continuations at a step. **Globally** it has no
lookahead and no backtracking, so an early decision it cannot revisit
determines the outcome. The error analysis is the sharpest evidence:
with chain-of-thought prompting, a large share of failures were already
doomed after the first thought, sometimes after the first few words.

A concrete instantiation answers four questions, and this list is the
part of the paper worth memorizing because it transfers to anything
search-shaped.

1. **Thought decomposition.** What is one step. Chain of thought leaves
   this ambiguous; here it is chosen per problem, and the sizing rule is
   the useful part: a thought should be small enough that the model can
   generate promising and diverse candidates, and big enough that the
   model can *evaluate* its prospects. One token is too small to
   evaluate; a whole book is too big to stay coherent.
2. **Thought generator.** Two strategies. Sample candidates
   independently from a chain-of-thought prompt, which suits a rich
   space where independent samples are naturally diverse. Or use a
   **propose prompt** that emits several candidates in one pass, which
   suits a constrained space, because generating them in the same
   context is what stops the model from repeating itself.
3. **State evaluator.** Also two strategies. **Value** each state on its
   own, producing a scalar or a coarse classification such as sure,
   maybe, or impossible, reasoned out through a few lookahead
   simulations plus commonsense elimination. Or **vote** across states by
   comparing them inside one prompt, which is the natural choice when
   success is hard to score in absolute terms, such as whether a passage
   is coherent. The paper notes voting across states is a step-wise form
   of self-consistency, and that valuations do not need to be perfect,
   only approximately helpful.
4. **Search algorithm.** Breadth-first search keeping the most promising
   states at each level, or depth-first search that follows the best
   candidate, prunes when the evaluator says a state is hopeless, and
   backtracks to the parent.

**The novel claim sits in the evaluator.** Search heuristics have
historically been either programmed by hand or learned from data. Here
the heuristic is the model deliberating in language about its own partial
solutions. That is the third option, and it is what makes the framework
apply to problems nobody can formalize, such as whether a piece of
writing is going somewhere.

Two properties follow. **Generality**: standard prompting, chain of
thought, self-consistency, and self-refinement are all special cases of
this framework, trees of restricted depth and breadth. **Modularity**:
the model, the decomposition, the generator, the evaluator, and the
search algorithm each vary independently, and none of them requires
training.

The ablations are more instructive than the results.

- **Removing backtracking collapses performance.** Keeping only the
  single most promising candidate and allowing overwrites, which is
  roughly greedy search, does poorly. Backtracking is not a refinement,
  it is the point.
- **Removing pruning is generally worse, but pruning is not safe.** The
  evaluator sometimes declared a state impossible when the puzzle had in
  fact been solved, because it did not recognize a rare word. Without
  pruning the search solved cases that pruning had cut off. A wrong
  confident judgment deletes the subtree containing the answer.
- **The output-selection step is a separate failure.** Reading out the
  oracle best state instead of the heuristically chosen one solved
  noticeably more cases. The search had found answers that the final
  selection step did not return.

## What problem it solves

Chain of thought made intermediate steps visible but kept them on a
single line, and self-consistency sampled several such lines without
letting them interact. This paper names both limits precisely: within one
chain there is **no local exploration** of alternative steps, and the
most-frequent heuristic that self-consistency relies on **only applies
when the output space is limited**. Neither method can revise an early
commitment, and neither can produce a good answer where the answer is a
passage rather than a value that can be counted.

Tree of Thoughts gives the model a place to put alternatives, a way to
compare them, and a policy for which to expand next. It is the move from
generating a solution to searching for one.

## Limitations

- **It is unnecessary for tasks the model already handles.** The paper
  puts this first in its own discussion. The three tasks were constructed
  specifically because they defeated the strongest model of the day.
- **It costs more than sampling**, and the modularity is offered as the
  mitigation: the depth, the breadth, and the evaluation budget are all
  knobs you set against your own cost ceiling.
- **It is not a drop-in.** Every one of the four questions is answered by
  you, per task. Chain of thought was a prompt you could copy;
  this is a program you have to design.
- **The evaluator is fallible in both directions**, and the paper
  demonstrates it. It can prune a solved state and it can keep a doomed
  one. Since the evaluator is also the pruning authority, its mistakes
  are not recoverable within the run.
- **Only three tasks**, all deliberately artificial. The paper is candid
  that real decision-making applications are where the question actually
  matters and that this work does not go there.

## What this means in practice

1. **You will rarely build "a Tree of Thoughts", and you will build its
   parts constantly.** Any time you generate several candidates, score
   them, keep the best few, and abandon a line that stopped looking
   promising, you have written this framework under another name. The
   four questions are the design checklist: what is a step, how do I
   produce candidates, how do I judge them, and what do I expand next. An
   agent that cannot answer the third and fourth is doing fan-out, not
   search.

2. **The evaluator is the system, and it is the part that will be
   wrong.** Everything downstream inherits its judgment, and pruning
   makes its mistakes permanent and silent. Three rules follow from the
   paper's own ablation. Prefer coarse buckets over fine scores, because
   the model can tell hopeless from promising far more reliably than it
   can produce a calibrated number. Do not prune on a single evaluation
   when the subtree is expensive to lose. And **log what you pruned**,
   with the reason, because the failure mode is invisible otherwise: a
   search that discards the answer looks exactly like a search that never
   found it.

3. **Size the thought unit on purpose, and use evaluability as the
   test.** If your evaluator cannot meaningfully distinguish two
   candidates, the unit is too small. If the model cannot produce a
   coherent candidate in one pass, it is too big. This is the same
   judgment as tool granularity and subtask size, and it is usually
   easier to get right by starting from what can be judged than from what
   can be generated.

4. **Choose the generator by how constrained the space is.** Independent
   samples for a rich space, one propose call for a narrow one. The
   second is the underused trick: candidates generated in a single
   context can see each other and therefore avoid duplicating each other,
   and it is one call instead of several. Independent sampling in a
   narrow space mostly buys you the same candidate repeatedly at full
   price.

5. **Choose value or vote by whether an absolute score is meaningful.**
   Feasibility, validity, and constraint satisfaction can be valued
   directly. Coherence, quality, and style usually cannot, and comparing
   candidates against each other in one prompt gives a far more stable
   signal than asking for a rating of each in isolation. A relative
   judgment is a much easier question than an absolute one.

6. **Cost compounds here, unlike in voting.** Self-consistency multiplies
   by a flat number of samples. A search multiplies nodes by generation
   plus evaluation, and evaluation is itself often sampled more than
   once, and the node count grows with both depth and breadth. Cap the
   total node budget rather than only the depth, which is what the paper
   does with its search-step limit. A tree with no global budget is an
   unbounded bill.

7. **Search pays only when the task has an early-commitment problem, and
   the error analysis is how you find out.** Look at where your failures
   occur. If they are decided at the first step and nothing downstream
   recovers, search is the right instrument. If failures are spread
   evenly, or the single-pass answer is already good, search buys
   latency and cost and nothing else.

8. **Evaluate the search and the readout separately.** The oracle
   ablation is the warning: the answer was inside the tree and the final
   step did not return it. Two distinct metrics, did the search contain a
   solution, and did the selection return it, or you will spend your
   effort tuning the wrong half.

9. **Backtracking is free only for thoughts.** This framework assumes a
   node can be abandoned at no cost, which is true when the node is text
   the model produced and nothing else happened. The moment a step calls
   a tool that writes a file, sends a message, or charges a card, backing
   out is no longer free and may not be possible. Keeping the tree
   external, in your own state, is what lets you snapshot, prune, and
   resume; an internal chain of reasoning gives you nothing to roll back
   to.

## What it leads to next

Nothing in this library extends it: within `01-reasoning` this is the end
of the line, and the reading order arrives here from 2201.11903 through
2203.11171. The paper itself leaves the more capable search algorithms as
future work.

The productive next step is sideways, into `02-acting-and-tools`. **ReAct
(2210.03629)** interleaves reasoning with actions against an environment,
which changes the economics described above: the nodes acquire side
effects, backtracking stops being free, and the evaluator gains something
better than its own judgment to work with, namely observations from the
world. Read this paper for the search machinery and that one for what
happens when the search is no longer confined to text.

`03-self-correction` is the other direction: linear revision of one
attempt rather than exploration of many. This paper notes that
self-refinement is itself a special case, a tree of limited breadth.

## Connection to the lab

`papers.yaml` maps this paper to `labs/track-1-patterns/05-orchestrator-workers`,
and the mapping is best read as **the lab being one level of this
paper's tree, executed once**.

Lab 05 has a lead call that reads a task and generates subtasks, a
validation step, workers that execute them, and a synthesis call. Set
that beside the four questions: the lead is the thought generator, the
subtasks are the thoughts, and synthesis is the readout. What lab 05
does not have is a **state evaluator** and a **search policy**. It plans
once, executes, and stops. That absence is the whole difference, and it
is why the lab is a workflow while this paper is a search.

**`validate_plan` is the deterministic half of a state evaluator.** The
lab's instruction is to treat the generated plan as untrusted input:
reject entries missing fields, drop duplicates, cap the count. This
paper's evaluator does the semantic half, judging whether a partial
solution is worth pursuing. The ablations argue for keeping both and
ordering them: the structural check is cheap and cannot be confidently
wrong, so run it first and spend model calls only on what survives.

**`max_subtasks` is a breadth limit.** The lab caps the plan because a
generated plan can be empty, enormous, or redundant. This paper caps
breadth per level and caps total search steps for exactly the same
reason, and the lab's own warning that cost grows quickly is the mild
version of what happens when the tree is allowed to grow in two
dimensions.

**`synthesize` is the readout, and the oracle ablation is the caution.**
The lab is right that synthesis is a real step and not a concatenation.
This paper adds the failure mode: a synthesis step can fail to surface
what the workers actually found. Worth testing on its own.

Lab 05's suggestion under going further, to let a worker request one
additional subtask and watch how fast a self-extending plan grows, is the
tree appearing on purpose. This paper is what that exercise turns into
once the growth is disciplined by an evaluator and a budget.

Elsewhere in the sibling repository:

- **Lab 04, parallelization.** The vote-across-states evaluator is

  reappear here as a scoring function rather than as a final answer.
- **Lab 06, evaluator-optimizer.** The same evaluator component in the

  what a search buys and what it costs.
- **Lab 12, the agent loop.** Where backtracking stops being free,
  because the steps touch the world. The design question this paper
  leaves you is which of your steps are reversible.
- **Lab 16, observability.** A search is the case where a plain trace of
  calls is not enough. The shape worth recording is the tree: which
  states were expanded, what each was scored, and above all which were
  pruned and why.
- **Lab 15, evaluation.** Two metrics, not one, per the oracle ablation:
  whether the search found a solution, and whether the readout returned
  it.
