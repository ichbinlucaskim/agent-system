# Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651>

Madaan, Tandon, Gupta, Hallinan, Gao, Wiegreffe, Alon, Dziri, Prabhumoye,
Yang, Gupta, Majumder, Hermann, Welleck, Yazdanbakhsh, Clark. Carnegie
Mellon, Allen Institute for AI, University of Washington, NVIDIA, UC San
Diego, Google Research. 2023.

## Core ideas

One model, three prompts, no training. Generate a draft, hand the draft
back to the **same** model for feedback, hand the feedback back to the
same model to produce a revision, and repeat until a stopping condition
fires. No supervised data, no reward model, no reinforcement learning, no
second model.

That is the entire method, and its plainness is the argument: iterative
refinement had previously meant training a refiner on domain-specific
data, or standing up external supervision. This shows the loop can be
closed inside one frozen model with three prompts.

Two structural details are worth noticing.

- **The refine step conditions on the whole history**, not just the last
  draft. The algorithm passes the input, then every draft and every piece
  of feedback so far. The model can see that it already tried something
  and what was said about it, which is what keeps the loop from cycling
  between two revisions.
- **The stopping condition is either a fixed iteration count or a signal
  the model itself emits.** There is no external judge deciding when the
  output is good enough.

**The result that matters is about feedback quality, not about the
loop.** The ablation compares specific actionable feedback against
generic feedback against none, and the ordering is consistent: specific
beats generic beats nothing. On one task the loop **fails outright**
without feedback, even though the model is still free to keep rewriting.

The paper defines the two words precisely, and the definitions are the
transferable part. Feedback is **actionable** when it contains a concrete
action likely to improve the output. It is **specific** when it points at
concrete phrases in the output to change. Their contrast is
`Avoid repeated calculations in the for loop` against
`Improve the efficiency of the code`. The second is not weaker advice, it
is a different kind of object: it names a goal the model already had.

Iterating helps, with **clearly diminishing returns**. Most of the
improvement is in the first pass or two.

## What problem it solves

A model's first output is a draft, and for tasks with multifaceted
objectives or hard-to-define goals — a dialogue response that must be
engaging and attentive, code that must be readable and fast — one pass
rarely satisfies all of them at once. The prior fix was to train a
refiner or buy external supervision, both of which cost data that most
tasks do not have.

The claim here is that the ability to *recognize* a shortcoming is
already present in a model that cannot avoid the shortcoming on the first
try. Nothing new is learned; the loop just gives the existing critical
ability a place to act.

## Limitations

- **It depends on the base model being good at instruction following and
  few-shot learning**, because both the critique and the revision are
  produced in context. The paper names this as its main limitation.
- **The feedback and the answer come from the same model.** If the model
  cannot see the flaw, the loop cannot find it, and the loop will still
  run and still report progress.
- Experiments used closed models whose training details are not
  published, and English-only datasets.
- No guard against a user steering the loop toward worse outputs on
  purpose.

## What this means in practice

1. **The loop is not the contribution; the feedback specification is.**
   Any two prompts in sequence will produce a refinement loop, and it
   will do close to nothing unless the critique names a location and an
   action. Write the feedback prompt as a checklist that forces both:
   what exactly is wrong, where exactly it is, and what specific change
   would fix it. Ask a model to say whether a draft is good and you have
   built the ablation that fails.

2. **The decisive question for this whole topic: is there a signal from
   outside the model's own opinion?** Here there is not, which bounds
   what the loop can do. If you have tests, a compiler, a schema
   validator, a linter, or a real reward, use it, and you are in
   2303.11366's shape instead. Reach for pure self-feedback when no such
   signal exists — prose, tone, style, structure — and be honest that you
   are trading a correctness guarantee for polish.

3. **Two iterations, then stop and measure.** The returns diminish fast.
   A loop that runs many passes by default is usually paying full price
   for the tail of a curve that flattened early. Make the count a
   measured parameter, not a constant you chose once.

4. **Keep the history in the refine call, as the algorithm does.**
   Feeding only the latest draft invites the model to undo a fix it made
   two rounds ago. The trajectory is what makes the loop monotone-ish
   rather than a random walk.

5. **Refinement is not free and it is serial.** Unlike voting in
   2203.11171, these calls cannot overlap: each one needs the previous
   output. So the cost is a multiple *and* the latency is a multiple.
   That is the trade against parallel sampling, and it is usually the
   deciding factor when a human is waiting.

6. **A self-graded score is not an evaluation result.** The loop's own
   scores are produced by the thing being scored. They are fine as a stop
   condition and worthless as a quality report. If a number leaves this
   loop and goes into a dashboard, it needs to come from somewhere else.

## What it leads to next

- **Reflexion (2303.11366)** is the same instinct with three things
  added: an external or simulated reward signal rather than pure
  self-opinion, an agent trajectory rather than a single output, and
  memory that persists across attempts. This paper refines one draft in
  place; that one retries the whole episode having written down what went
  wrong.

The library files these two as contemporaries with no direction of
influence claimed. Read them as two settings of one dial: how much
outside signal you have.

## Connection to the lab

`papers.yaml` maps this to
`labs/track-1-patterns/06-evaluator-optimizer`, and this is close to a
direct implementation.

**The lab's opening condition is the criterion for using this pattern at
all**: it applies when you can articulate what good looks like better
than you can produce it in one shot. That sentence is the practical test.
If you cannot articulate it, the feedback prompt has nothing to say and
you get the generic-feedback ablation.

**`CRITERIA` is this paper's specificity finding turned into a
deliverable.** The lab asks for a list of specific, independently
gradeable checks and says outright that vague criteria are the usual
reason the pattern fails. That is the ablation restated as instruction:
naming every requirement, staying under a length, avoiding the passive
voice are gradeable; being good is not.

**The lab adds the thing this paper leaves to the model: an explicit
budget.** `is_done` returns both whether to stop and which condition
fired, a success threshold or an exhausted iteration count. This paper's
stop condition is a fixed count or a signal the model emits about itself,
which is the weaker of the two designs, and the lab's version is what you
should actually ship.

**`refine` tracking the best draft seen is a correction this paper's
diminishing-returns curve implies but does not state.** Scores do not
rise monotonically, and a later pass can be worse than an earlier one.
Returning the highest-scoring draft with its score and its iteration
number is what makes the loop's behaviour inspectable rather than
merely final.

The lab also names the failure mode that decides whether you want this
pattern or lab 04: if the second call does not see the critique, the loop
is just resampling, and resampling is better done concurrently. That is
the same boundary as point five above, stated as a design rule.
